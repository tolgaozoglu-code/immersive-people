Attendance is the easiest number in our field to collect and the least informative. A turnstile counts bodies, not experiences. Two exhibitions can admit the same ten thousand people while one changes how a fraction of them see the world and the other is forgotten by dinner. If immersive culture wants to be taken seriously by institutions and funders, it needs measures that can tell these two outcomes apart.

## Start from a model of the visit

The most durable framework in museum studies remains John Falk and Lynn Dierking's contextual model of learning: what a visitor takes away is produced by the interaction of a personal context (motivations, prior knowledge), a sociocultural context (who they came with, who they meet), and a physical context (the designed environment). Measurement that samples only the physical context, footfall and dwell time, is measuring one third of the phenomenon.

In practice this means pairing observation with conversation. Timing-and-tracking studies show where attention pools and where it drains; brief exit interviews reveal whether the pooling meant fascination or confusion.

## Behaviour is testimony

Long before anyone fills in a survey, engagement is visible. Visitors who are engaged slow down, point, and talk to each other about the content rather than about logistics. Social behaviour is a particularly honest signal in shared experiences: sociologists since Durkheim have described the charge of collective effervescence, the energy of attending to something together, and you can watch it form or fail in a room. We log simple observable events, returns to an earlier station, full sits through a cycle, spontaneous explanation between strangers, because they are hard to fake and cheap to count.

## The measure that matters most arrives weeks later

Memory research is blunt about forgetting: most of an experience decays fast, and what survives is what was encoded deeply. This makes the delayed follow-up the single most valuable instrument we use. Contact a sample of visitors three or six weeks later and ask what they still see when they close their eyes, what they told someone else about, whether anything changed what they read, watched or visited next. The answers are qualitative, but they separate spectacle from impact with uncomfortable clarity. An experience people can still narrate a month later, in their own words and in the first person, did its job.

## Define impact before you instrument anything

The temptation of immersive spaces is that they can be instrumented endlessly: sensors, heatmaps, dashboards. Data without a prior definition of success degenerates into vanity metrics. Our practice is to agree with the institution, before opening, on two or three claims the experience is trying to make true, for example that visitors leave able to explain why a heritage site matters, or that strangers interact inside the work. Every instrument then exists to test a claim, and anything that tests nothing is switched off.

Attendance still gets reported, of course. But it belongs in the finance meeting, not the impact report. What deserves the word engagement is attention, memory and change, and all three can be measured if you decide to.
